# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet decides to flee across the sea to escape a divine commission. His desperate attempt to hide from the Almighty leads to a terrifying storm and an underwater rescue. Discover how God's relentless mercy pursues both a rebellious messenger and a wicked city.

## The Problem of Assyrian Evil
Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian Empire and a city of immense size (Jonah 1:2, 3:3). The Assyrians were notorious for their extreme cruelty and violence against their enemies (Nahum 3:1,19). They posed a constant threat to the security of Israel and the surrounding nations (2 Kings 15:19). The wickedness of the Ninevites had reached a point where it could no longer be ignored by heaven (Jonah 1:2). The people lived in a state of spiritual darkness and moral decay (Jonah 4:11). They were far from the laws and the knowledge of the God of Israel (Jonah 1:9, 3:5). The political environment was dominated by a superpower that used terror to maintain its control (Nahum 3:1). The problem was that the extreme evil of the people of Nineveh had come up before the LORD and required a divine warning of destruction (Jonah 1:2).

## Jonah's Prophetic Condition
Jonah was the son of Amittai and a recognized prophet in the land of Israel (Jonah 1:1; 2 Kings 14:25). He had previously prophesied the restoration of Israel's borders during the reign of Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25). Jonah did nothing special to get God's attention before this specific call to Nineveh (Jonah 1:1). He was not praying or fasting in the temple when the word of the LORD came to him (Jonah 1:1). He was simply a servant of the LORD dwelling in his own country (Jonah 4:2).

## The Divine Calling
The word of the LORD came to Jonah directly on two separate occasions to command him to preach to the city of Nineveh.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me."
>
> — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

After Jonah tried to run away, the word came a second time.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you**."
>
> — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice
Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and his nationalistic pride (Jonah 4:1-2). He risked his life by entering a city known for its violence and hostility (Jonah 1:2; Nahum 3:1). He also risked his reputation as a prophet if the predicted destruction did not occur (Jonah 4:2). He sacrificed his desire for vengeance against the enemies of Israel to obey the command of the LORD (Jonah 4:2). He had to leave his homeland to travel to a foreign and dangerous land (Jonah 3:3).

## Jonah's Initial Response
Jonah initially rebelled against the calling and refused to go to Nineveh (Jonah 1:3). He rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going in the opposite direction of his assignment (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went down into the ship to hide from his responsibility (Jonah 1:3). Jonah admitted later that he fled because he knew the LORD was too merciful (Jonah 4:2).

## Jonah's Eventual Reaction
Jonah boarded a ship to Tarshish, but the LORD hurled a great wind upon the sea (Jonah 1:3-4). While the mariners were afraid and crying out to their gods, Jonah was fast asleep in the inner part of the ship (Jonah 1:5). The captain woke him and urged him to call out to his God (Jonah 1:6). The sailors cast lots to discover who was responsible for the storm, and the lot fell on Jonah (Jonah 1:7). He confessed that he was a Hebrew who feared the LORD and was fleeing from **His** presence (Jonah 1:9-10). He told the men to hurl him into the sea so that the tempest would quiet down (Jonah 1:12). The sailors eventually threw him overboard, and the sea ceased from its raging (Jonah 1:15). Jonah was swallowed by a great fish that the LORD had appointed (Jonah 1:17). He remained in the belly of the fish for three days and three nights (Jonah 1:17). From within the fish, Jonah prayed and acknowledged that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:1,9). The fish vomited him out upon the dry land at the command of the LORD (Jonah 2:10). When the word came the second time, Jonah arose and went to Nineveh (Jonah 3:3). He walked into the city and proclaimed that in forty days it would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). He waited outside the city to see if God would still destroy it (Jonah 4:5). He became angry when the LORD relented from the disaster (Jonah 4:1). He complained about a plant that provided him shade and then withered (Jonah 4:6-9). Finally, he listened as the LORD explained **His** compassion for the many people and animals in Nineveh (Jonah 4:10-11). Jonah's journey was one of resistance, discipline, and eventual, though reluctant, obedience (Jonah 3:3, 4:1).

## The Divine Miracle
God performed a miracle of preservation and an even greater miracle of mass repentance. **He** saved Jonah from the depths and turned the hearts of a wicked city toward **Himself**.

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights**. . . And the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land. . . And **the people of Nineveh believed God**. They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them. . . When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them**, and **He** did not do it.
>
> — Jonah 1:17, 2:10, 3:5,10 (ESV)

The LORD fulfilled **His** promise to have Jonah **call out against it** by ensuring the message was heard and responded to (Jonah 3:2,10).

## The Lasting Result
The miracle resulted in the survival of over one hundred and twenty thousand people in Nineveh (Jonah 4:11). The king and the citizens repented in sackcloth and ashes, turning from their evil ways and violence (Jonah 3:6-8). The impending judgment was averted, and the city was spared from destruction (Jonah 3:10). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD exceedingly, offering sacrifices and making vows (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself experienced the power of God's deliverance from the pit and the depth of **His** patience (Jonah 2:6, 4:10). The story became a sign for future generations, illustrating that God's mercy extends even to the most undeserving nations (Matthew 12:41). It demonstrated that the word of the LORD is effective and that **He** desires repentance rather than death (Ezekiel 18:23). The result was a profound demonstration of the sovereignty and compassion of the God of all the earth (Jonah 4:11).

## Conclusion
Jonah faced the problem of a wicked city and his own deep-seated prejudice against them. He was a prophet limited by his nationalistic views and his attempt to flee from God. Despite his rebellion and his anger at God's mercy, the LORD provided a great fish to save him and a message to save Nineveh. Jonah reluctantly responded to the second call and witnessed the entire city turn to God. The LORD intervened to spare a repentant people and to teach **His** servant about the true nature of compassion. Jonah sacrificed his comfort and his pride, but he gained a witness to the awesome power of God's grace. This story proves that we can trust the LORD to be faithful to **His** character even when we fail. **He** is a gracious God **Who** is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.
