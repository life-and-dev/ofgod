# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet receives a divine command to warn a wicked enemy city. Instead of obeying, he flees in the opposite direction on a ship. A terrifying storm and a massive sea creature intervene to redirect his path toward a destination he desperately wants to avoid.

## The Wickedness of Nineveh

Jonah was the son of Amittai and a prophet from Gath-hepher in Israel (2 Kings 14:25). He lived during the reign of Jeroboam II when the northern kingdom was expanding its borders (2 Kings 14:23-25). However, the Assyrian Empire with its capital at Nineveh was a rising and brutal threat to the region (Jonah 1:2). Nineveh was an exceedingly great city but was known for its extreme wickedness and violence (Jonah 1:2, 3:8). The LORD observed that their evil had come up before Him (Jonah 1:2). The people of Nineveh were pagans who did not know their right hand from their left (Jonah 4:11). They were enemies of Israel and represented a culture of cruelty (Jonah 1:2). Jonah knew that God was gracious and might relent from punishing them if they repented (Jonah 4:2). The problem was that a great city was facing imminent divine destruction because of its persistent wickedness (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Prophetic Condition

Jonah was a recognized prophet of the LORD in Israel (2 Kings 14:25). He did not perform any recorded ritual like fasting or special sacrifice to receive this specific word (Jonah 1:1). The word of the LORD simply came to him while he was in his country (Jonah 1:1, 4:2). He was a Hebrew who feared the LORD, the God of heaven, Who made the sea and the dry land (Jonah 1:9). God initiated the contact without any prior action from Jonah to seek a new mission (Jonah 1:1-2).

## The Divine Calling

The word of the LORD came directly to Jonah with a specific command to go to a foreign city.

> "Arise, **go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

Later, after Jonah's attempt to flee, the LORD spoke to him a second time with the same urgent message.

> "Arise, **go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it the message that I tell you**." — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Cost of Obedience

Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort, national pride, and his own reputation as a prophet (Jonah 1:3, 4:2). By preaching to Nineveh, he risked being seen as a traitor to his own people who suffered under Assyrian threats (Jonah 1:2). He had to abandon his secure life in Israel to travel to a dangerous and hostile foreign capital (Jonah 3:3). He also risked his own sense of justice by offering mercy to a people he believed deserved only wrath (Jonah 4:1-2).

## Jonah's Flight From God

Jonah immediately rebelled against the divine command (Jonah 1:3). He rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went into the ship to go away from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He deliberately chose the opposite geographical direction to avoid the mission God had assigned to him (Jonah 1:2-3).

## Resistance and Eventual Submission

While Jonah was fleeing on the ship, the LORD hurled a great wind upon the sea (Jonah 1:4). Jonah went to the inner part of the ship and fell fast asleep during the tempest (Jonah 1:5). The sailors cast lots to find the cause of the storm and the lot fell on Jonah (Jonah 1:7). He confessed his flight and told the sailors to hurl him into the sea so it would quiet down (Jonah 1:12). Once in the water, the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow him (Jonah 1:17). Jonah remained in the belly of the fish for three days and three nights (Jonah 1:17).

From the belly of the fish, Jonah prayed and acknowledged that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:1,9). After the fish vomited him onto dry land, he obeyed the second call and went to Nineveh (Jonah 2:10, 3:3). He walked into the city and proclaimed that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). When the city repented and God relented, Jonah became exceedingly angry (Jonah 4:1). He complained to the LORD that He was too merciful (Jonah 4:2). He went out of the city and sat under a booth to see what would happen (Jonah 4:5).

The LORD then used a plant, a worm, and a scorching wind to teach Jonah about compassion (Jonah 4:6-8). Jonah was glad for the plant but angry when it died (Jonah 4:6,9). The LORD questioned why Jonah pitied a plant he did not make but resented mercy for a city of 120,000 people (Jonah 4:10-11). Despite his initial rebellion and his later resentment, Jonah eventually fulfilled the task of delivering God's warning (Jonah 3:3-4). His reaction revealed a man struggling with the boundary between his own prejudices and the boundless mercy of the LORD (Jonah 4:2,11).

## The Miracle of Preservation and Repentance

God used a series of supernatural events to preserve Jonah and convert a pagan city. The most significant miracle was the preservation of Jonah and the mass repentance of Nineveh.

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights**. . . And **the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land**. . . And the people of Nineveh believed God. . . When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it**. — Jonah 1:17, 2:10, 3:5,10 (ESV)

## The Survival of a City

The result of God's intervention was the total preservation of Nineveh and its inhabitants (Jonah 3:10). The king and all the people turned from their evil ways and violence (Jonah 3:6-8). This mass repentance prevented a catastrophic disaster for over 120,000 people and much cattle (Jonah 4:11). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD exceedingly and offered sacrifices (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself was rescued from certain death in the sea (Jonah 2:6). Though Jonah struggled with the outcome, the mission was a complete success in terms of divine mercy (Jonah 3:10). The story became a lasting sign to future generations of God's willingness to forgive even the most wicked nations (Matthew 12:41). It demonstrated that God's compassion extends far beyond the borders of Israel to all people who repent (Jonah 4:11).

## Conclusion

Nineveh faced destruction due to its extreme wickedness, and Jonah failed by attempting to flee God's command. He preferred the destruction of his enemies over their salvation. Despite his rebellion and small-mindedness, the LORD followed through with His purpose. Through a great storm, a fish, and a plant, God demonstrated His sovereignty and mercy. Jonah eventually preached the message, and the entire city repented and was spared. Jonah risked his life in the sea and his reputation as a prophet. However, he gained a deeper understanding of the heart of God. The story shows that God's faithfulness is not limited by human failure or prejudice. He is the God Who pursues the runaway prophet and the wicked city alike to bring about salvation.
