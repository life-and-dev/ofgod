# Jonah: a Second Chance

A prophet receives a command to warn a brutal enemy city of impending judgment. Instead of obeying, he boards a ship to the opposite end of the world. Witness how God uses a storm and a great fish to bring **His** runaway messenger back to **His** task.

## The Problem of Nineveh's Wickedness

Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian Empire and was known for its extreme cruelty and violence (Nahum 3:1). The wickedness of the city had become so great that it had come up before the LORD (Jonah 1:2). The political environment was one of constant threat as the Assyrians were a dominant and aggressive power in the ancient Near East (2 Kings 15:19). Israel had reason to fear and despise these people who would eventually lead them into captivity (2 Kings 17:6). The prophet Jonah was a servant of the LORD from Gath-hepher in Galilee (2 Kings 14:25). He lived during the reign of Jeroboam II when Israel was experiencing a period of expansion and prosperity (2 Kings 14:23-25). The problem was that Nineveh's evil required a divine warning of destruction, but the messenger was unwilling to see his enemies receive mercy (Jonah 1:2, 4:2).

## Jonah's Reluctant Condition

Jonah was a recognized prophet in Israel who had previously spoken words of success for the kingdom (2 Kings 14:25). He was the son of Amittai (Jonah 1:1). He did not perform any special acts like fasting or visiting the temple to get God's attention (Jonah 1:1). He was simply in a state of being a servant of the LORD when the word came to him (Jonah 1:1). He was a man who understood the character of God but allowed his nationalistic prejudice to interfere with his obedience (Jonah 4:2).

## The Divine Calling

The word of the LORD came directly to Jonah with a clear command to go and preach against the city of Nineveh.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me."
>
> — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

After Jonah's flight and rescue, the LORD called him a second time with the same mission.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you**."
>
> — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice

Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and his safety to travel to a hostile foreign city (Jonah 3:3). He risked his reputation as a prophet of Israel by delivering a message of mercy to their enemies (Jonah 4:2). He had to give up his own hatred and desire for Nineveh's destruction to fulfill God's purpose (Jonah 4:2). He sacrificed his own sense of justice for the sake of God's compassion (Jonah 4:10-11).

## Jonah's Initial Response

Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish to get away from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to the opposite direction of Nineveh (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went down into the ship, hoping to escape his calling (Jonah 1:3). He showed a complete lack of trust in God's plan and a deliberate rebellion against **His** command (Jonah 1:3). He even fell into a deep sleep during a life-threatening storm while the pagan sailors were crying out to their gods (Jonah 1:5-6).

## Jonah's Eventual Reaction

After being confronted by the sailors and the lot falling on him, Jonah admitted he was a Hebrew who feared the LORD (Jonah 1:9-10). He told the men to hurl him into the sea to quiet the storm for them (Jonah 1:12). While in the belly of the great fish, Jonah prayed to the LORD and acknowledged that salvation belongs to **Him** (Jonah 2:1,9). He promised to pay what he had vowed and to sacrifice with a voice of thanksgiving (Jonah 2:9).

Once he was vomited out onto dry land, Jonah obeyed the second call of the LORD (Jonah 3:3). He entered Nineveh and walked for a day, proclaiming the message of judgment (Jonah 3:4). He cried out that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). Even though he still harbored anger when God relented (Jonah 4:1), he had performed the task **He** set before him (Jonah 3:10).

He sat outside the city to see what would happen, still hoping for its destruction (Jonah 4:5). He interacted with the LORD regarding a plant that gave him shade, which **He** used to teach Jonah about compassion (Jonah 4:6-11). He listened as the LORD explained **His** pity for the 120,000 people and much cattle in Nineveh (Jonah 4:11). Jonah's story ends with him being a vessel of God's mercy despite his own flawed heart (Jonah 4:11). He demonstrated that even a reluctant and rebellious prophet can be used by the LORD to accomplish **His** will (Jonah 3:10).

## The Divine Miracle

The LORD performed several miracles to bring Jonah to repentance and to save the city of Nineveh (Jonah 1:4,17, 3:10). **He** hurled a great wind, appointed a great fish, and caused a whole city to repent (Jonah 1:4,17, 3:5).

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.** . . And the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land. . . And the people of Nineveh believed God. They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them. . . When **God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, God relented of the disaster** that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it.
>
> — Jonah 1:17, 2:10, 3:5,10 (ESV)

In these miracles, the LORD fulfilled **His** purpose to **call out against it** and to deal with the fact that **their evil has come up before Me** by bringing them to repentance (Jonah 1:2, 3:10).

## The Lasting Result

The miracle of Jonah's rescue and preaching resulted in the preservation of the entire city of Nineveh (Jonah 3:10). From the king to the animals, the whole population fasted and turned from their evil ways (Jonah 3:6-8). The king of Nineveh issued a decree for everyone to call out mightily to God (Jonah 3:7-8). This mass repentance prevented the immediate destruction of the city (Jonah 3:10).

Jonah himself learned a profound lesson about the depth of God's mercy which extends even to those outside of Israel (Jonah 4:10-11). His experience in the fish became a sign that Jesus would later use to point to his own death and resurrection (Matthew 12:39-41). The story served as a rebuke to Israel's own lack of repentance despite having many prophets (Matthew 12:41). It established the LORD as the God of all nations **Who** desires that all should turn and live (Ezekiel 18:23).

## Conclusion

Jonah faced the problem of a wicked enemy city and his own stubborn refusal to deliver God's warning. He was a prophet limited by his own prejudice and desire for vengeance. However, the LORD promised to deliver a message to Nineveh through him. Jonah attempted to sacrifice his calling by fleeing, but he ended up sacrificing his comfort in the depths of the sea. God intervened by preserving him in a fish and sovereignly moving the hearts of the Ninevites. Jonah sacrificed his pride and his hatred, but he gained a witness to the greatest revival in history and a place in the lineage of prophetic signs. His story proves that we can trust the LORD to be faithful even when we are unfaithful. **He** is the God of the second chance **Who** never forsakes **His** purpose.
