# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet receives a mission to warn a brutal enemy city, but he chooses to run in the opposite direction. His attempt to escape leads to a terrifying storm and an impossible survival at sea. Discover how God's persistent mercy overcomes rebellion to save the city.

## The Problem of a Wicked City

Jonah the son of Amittai was a prophet from Gath-hepher who lived during the reign of Jeroboam II in Israel (2 Kings 14:25). The nation of Israel was enjoying a period of territorial expansion and relative prosperity, yet it remained spiritually distant from the LORD (2 Kings 14:23-27). To the north, the Assyrian Empire was known for its extreme cruelty and violent conquests, with Nineveh as one of its most prominent and wicked cities (Nahum 3:1-3; Jonah 1:2). The political environment was one of constant threat from these brutal neighbors (Nahum 3:19). The people of Nineveh were practicing great evil that had come up before God (Jonah 1:2). The problem was that the wickedness of Nineveh had reached a tipping point, demanding either divine judgment or a message of repentance to avoid total destruction (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Humble Condition

Jonah was a recognized prophet of the LORD in Israel (2 Kings 14:25). He did nothing special like a sacrifice, prayer, or ceremony to get God's attention before the word of the LORD came to him (Jonah 1:1). He was simply a servant of God to whom the divine word was revealed (Jonah 1:1). There is no record of Jonah seeking this specific mission to Nineveh (Jonah 1:1-2). He was likely going about his normal duties in Israel when he was called to go to the heart of the enemy's empire (Jonah 1:1-2).

## The Divine Calling

The word of the LORD came to Jonah with a direct command to confront the wickedness of the great city.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

After his rescue from the sea, the LORD spoke to him a second time.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you.**" — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice

Jonah risked his reputation and his national pride by preaching to a hated enemy (Jonah 4:1-2). He sacrificed his comfort and safety to travel to a violent foreign capital (Jonah 3:3). He also risked the potential backlash from his own people for offering mercy to their oppressors (Jonah 4:2). By obeying, he had to give up his own desire for vengeance against the Assyrians (Jonah 4:1-3). He sacrificed his anonymity and his safety among the mariners to confess his rebellion (Jonah 1:9,12).

## Jonah's Initial Response

Jonah immediately rebelled and fled in the opposite direction (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish to escape from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He preferred a life as a fugitive on the sea rather than obeying the command to go to Nineveh (Jonah 1:3-4). His refusal was rooted in his knowledge that God is gracious and might relent from disaster (Jonah 4:2). He chose to pay a fare to run away rather than receiving the free grace of obedience (Jonah 1:3).

## Jonah's Faithful Reaction

When the LORD hurled a great wind upon the sea, Jonah was found sleeping in the inner part of the ship (Jonah 1:4-5). He eventually confessed to the mariners that he was a Hebrew who feared the LORD, the God of heaven, Who made the sea and the dry land (Jonah 1:9). He admitted that the storm was his fault and told them to hurl him into the sea so that it would quiet down for them (Jonah 1:12). After being swallowed by a great fish, Jonah prayed from its belly, acknowledging his distress and vowing to pay what he had vowed (Jonah 2:1-9). He recognized that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:9).

When the fish vomited him onto dry land and the LORD called him again, Jonah arose and went to Nineveh according to the word of the LORD (Jonah 3:1-3). He entered the city and proclaimed that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). Even after the city repented and God spared them, Jonah reacted with anger and displeasure (Jonah 4:1). He made a booth for himself outside the city to see what would become of it (Jonah 4:5). He struggled with God's compassion for his enemies, yet he remained under the instruction of the LORD as He taught him about His pity for all people (Jonah 4:6-11). Jonah's eventual reaction was one of reluctant obedience followed by a deep personal struggle with the character of God (Jonah 3:3, 4:1-4). He demonstrated that one can obey God's command while still having much to learn about His heart (Jonah 4:10-11).

## The Divine Miracle

God performed the miracle of preserving Jonah in the fish and then the greater miracle of a mass repentance in Nineveh.

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights. . . . And the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land. . . . So Jonah arose and went to Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD. . . . Jonah began to go into the city, going a day's journey. And he called out, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!" And **the people of Nineveh believed God.** They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them. . . . When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them**, and He did not do it. — Jonah 1:17, 2:10, 3:3-5,10 (ESV)

In these events, God ensured Jonah would **call out against it** and fulfill the mission to Nineveh (Jonah 3:3). He also performed a miracle with a plant and a worm to teach Jonah about His mercy (Jonah 4:6-8).

## The Lasting Result

The miracle resulted in the survival of over one hundred and twenty thousand people who did not know their right hand from their left (Jonah 4:11). The king and the people of Nineveh turned from their evil ways and from the violence in their hands (Jonah 3:8-10). This repentance delayed the judgment on the Assyrian capital for several generations (Jonah 3:10). The mariners on the ship also came to fear the LORD exceedingly, offering sacrifices and making vows (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself became a sign to the Ninevites and later to the generation of Jesus (Luke 11:30). The result was a profound demonstration that God's mercy extends beyond the borders of Israel to all nations (Jonah 4:11).

## Conclusion

Jonah faced the problem of a wicked enemy city and his own stubborn rebellion against God's mercy. He was limited by his prejudice and his desire for judgment rather than grace. However, the LORD promised that Nineveh would be warned through Jonah's voice. Jonah responded initially with flight and later with reluctant preaching after God's miraculous intervention. God intervened by saving Jonah through a great fish and transforming the hearts of the Ninevites. The result was the salvation of a city and a lesson in God's universal compassion. Jonah sacrificed his pride and comfort, but he gained a front-row seat to the power of God to change hearts. His story proves that we can trust the LORD to be faithful to His mission of mercy, even when we fail.
