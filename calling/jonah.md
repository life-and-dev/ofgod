# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet receives a mission to warn a brutal enemy city of impending judgment. Instead of obeying, he boards a ship headed in the opposite direction. Discover how God uses a storm and a great fish to bring a runaway messenger back to his calling.

## The Problem of a Wicked City
The word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai (Jonah 1:1). God observed that the evil of Nineveh had come up before Him (Jonah 1:2). Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, yet it was filled with wickedness and violence (Jonah 1:2, 3:3,8). During the reign of Jeroboam II, Israel enjoyed prosperity and expansion, but the rising power of Assyria posed a significant threat (2 Kings 14:23-25). The Assyrians were known for their extreme cruelty and military dominance in the region. Their capital, Nineveh, was a symbol of oppressive power and moral decay (Nahum 3:1). Jonah was a prophet from Gath-hepher in Galilee who had previously prophesied Israel's restoration (2 Kings 14:25). The problem was that the people of Nineveh were so wicked that God determined to overthrow the city unless they repented of their evil ways (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Prophetic Condition
Jonah was a recognized prophet of the LORD in Israel (Jonah 1:1; 2 Kings 14:25). He did not perform any special fast or sacrifice to receive God's attention (Jonah 1:1). The word of the LORD simply came to him while he was in his own country (Jonah 1:1, 4:2). He was a servant of the LORD who was suddenly interrupted by a direct and challenging command from God (Jonah 1:1-2).

## The Divine Calling
God spoke directly to Jonah to warn him of the coming judgment on Nineveh and to command him to preach.

> Now the word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:1-2 (ESV)

> Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time, saying, "**Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it the message that I tell you.**" — Jonah 3:1-2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice
Jonah sacrificed his comfort and safety by eventually entering the capital of Israel's enemies (Jonah 3:3). He risked his reputation as a prophet, fearing that if God relented, his prophecy of destruction would not come to pass (Jonah 4:2). He also sacrificed his personal peace, as he was deeply displeased with God's mercy toward the Gentiles (Jonah 4:1). He risked his life by asking to be thrown into a raging sea to save the sailors (Jonah 1:12).

## Jonah's Initial Response
Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship, paying the fare to go away from his calling (Jonah 1:3). He deliberately chose the opposite direction to avoid the mission God had given him (Jonah 1:3). He believed he could escape the reach of the LORD by traveling to the ends of the known world (Jonah 1:3,10).

## Jonah's Eventual Reaction
While on the ship, Jonah went to sleep during a violent storm sent by God (Jonah 1:4-5). When the sailors cast lots, the lot fell on him, and he admitted he was fleeing from the LORD (Jonah 1:7-10). He told the sailors to hurl him into the sea to quiet the storm (Jonah 1:12). After being swallowed by a great fish, Jonah prayed from its belly, acknowledging that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:1-9). When the fish vomited him onto dry land, he finally obeyed the second calling (Jonah 2:10; 3:3). He traveled through Nineveh for a day, proclaiming that the city would be overthrown in forty days (Jonah 3:4). Even after the city repented, Jonah reacted with anger and sat outside the city to see what would happen (Jonah 4:1,5). He argued with God about His character, yet he remained in the place where God could continue to teach him through the plant and the worm (Jonah 4:2-11). He eventually accepted the lesson of God's compassion for all people (Jonah 4:10-11).

## The Divine Miracle
God performed miracles by saving Jonah from the sea and by transforming the hearts of the Ninevites (Jonah 1:17, 3:10). He appointed a great fish and a plant to demonstrate His sovereignty and mercy (Jonah 1:17; 4:6).

> And the LORD **appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah**. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights. . . And the LORD spoke to the fish, and it **vomited Jonah out upon the dry land**. — Jonah 1:17; 2:10 (ESV)

> When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it.** — Jonah 3:10 (ESV)

In these miracles, God fulfilled the promise that Jonah would **call out against it** and that the message would be heard by the city (Jonah 1:2; 3:2,10).

## The Lasting Result
The miracle resulted in the total repentance of Nineveh, from the king to the animals (Jonah 3:5-8). Over 120,000 people were spared from destruction (Jonah 4:11). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD and offered sacrifices (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself learned the depth of God's compassion for all nations (Jonah 4:10-11). The story served as a sign for future generations, including Jesus' own ministry (Matthew 12:39-41). The repentance of a pagan city stood as a witness against the unfaithfulness of Israel (Matthew 12:41).

## Conclusion
Jonah faced the problem of Nineveh's wickedness and his own prejudice against them. He limited himself by fleeing from God's presence, yet the LORD'S purpose prevailed through a storm and a fish. Jonah responded with a prayer of surrender and eventually preached the message, leading to a miraculous national repentance. God intervened to save both the runaway prophet and the repentant city. Jonah sacrificed his comfort, his reputation, and his pride, but he gained a front-row seat to one of the greatest displays of God's mercy in history. His story proves that we can trust the LORD to be faithful even when we are reluctant or failing. He is a gracious God Who desires that none should perish.
