# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet attempts to outrun the Almighty rather than deliver a message of warning to his enemies. His journey takes him to the depths of the ocean and the heart of a violent empire. Discover how God's mercy overcomes even the most stubborn rebellion.

## The Wickedness of Nineveh

The city of Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian Empire and was known for its extreme cruelty and violence (Nahum 3:1). Its people were a constant threat to the survival of Israel and the surrounding nations (2 Kings 15:19). The wickedness of the city had risen up before the face of the LORD (Jonah 1:2). Nineveh was a city of blood, full of lies and pillage (Nahum 3:1). Its leaders were famous for their brutal treatment of captives and their relentless pursuit of conquest (Nahum 3:19). The political environment was one of terror as Assyria dominated the ancient world with an iron hand (Nahum 3:19). There was no healing for its bruise and its wound was grievous (Nahum 3:19). All who heard news of Nineveh clapped their hands over its impending fall (Nahum 3:19). The problem was that Nineveh's persistent evil had provoked divine judgment and its destruction was imminent unless the city repented (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Prophetic Condition

Jonah the son of Amittai was an established prophet from Gath-hepher in the territory of Zebulun (2 Kings 14:25). He had previously spoken the word of the LORD to King Jeroboam II concerning the restoration of Israel's borders (2 Kings 14:25). Jonah did nothing special like a sacrifice or a fast to initiate this specific encounter (Jonah 1:1). He was already a servant of the LORD when the word came to him (Jonah 1:1). His position was one of national respect and religious authority (2 Kings 14:25).

## The Divine Calling

The word of the LORD came to Jonah with a clear command to confront the greatest city of his day.

> Now the word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, "Arise, **go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:1-2 (ESV)

After Jonah's first attempt to flee, the LORD spoke to him again.

> Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time, saying, "Arise, **go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it the message that I tell you.**" — Jonah 3:1-2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice

Jonah had to sacrifice his nationalistic pride and his desire for the destruction of Israel's enemies (Jonah 4:1-2). He risked his reputation as a prophet of Israel by offering hope to a pagan nation (2 Kings 14:25; Jonah 4:2). He sacrificed his safety by entering the heart of a city known for its violence (Jonah 3:3; Nahum 3:1). He risked being seen as a traitor to his own people by warning their oppressors (Jonah 4:2).

## Jonah's Initial Response

Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went down into it to go with them away from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He chose to rebel against the clear command of the LORD because he knew God was gracious and merciful (Jonah 4:2). He preferred his own death to the salvation of Nineveh (Jonah 1:12, 4:3).

## Jonah's Eventual Reaction

After the LORD hurled a great wind upon the sea, Jonah was eventually cast into the depths (Jonah 1:4,15). From the belly of a great fish, he prayed to the LORD and acknowledged that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:1,9). When the fish vomited Jonah out upon the dry land, he obeyed the second call to go to Nineveh (Jonah 2:10, 3:3).

Jonah entered the city and began to call out the message of judgment (Jonah 3:4). He cried out that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). Even after the city repented, Jonah's heart remained bitter (Jonah 4:1). He went out of the city and sat to the east to see what would become of it (Jonah 4:5). He was exceedingly angry that the LORD had relented from the disaster **He** had threatened (Jonah 4:1-2).

Jonah fainted from the heat and asked that he might die (Jonah 4:8). He argued with the LORD about his right to be angry over a plant that withered (Jonah 4:9). Despite his resentment and his personal failures, Jonah became the instrument through which an entire empire heard the word of the LORD (Jonah 3:5). Jonah eventually recorded his own rebellion and the LORD'S correction for future generations (Jonah 1-4). His life proved that God can use a reluctant and failing servant to accomplish **His** divine purpose (Jonah 3:10). Jonah trusted the LORD with his life by being cast into the sea and entering the hostile city (Jonah 1:12, 3:3). Though his attitude was flawed, his feet eventually followed the path the LORD had set for him (Jonah 3:3). He learned that the LORD'S compassion extends beyond the borders of Israel (Jonah 4:11).

## The Divine Miracle

The LORD performed the miracle of preserving Jonah in the sea and bringing a massive city to its knees in repentance (Jonah 1:17, 3:10).

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights. . . And the LORD spoke to the fish, and **it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land**. — Jonah 1:17, 2:10 (ESV)

In Nineveh, the LORD moved the hearts of the people to respond to Jonah's warning (Jonah 3:5).

> And the people of Nineveh believed God. They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them. . . When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it.** — Jonah 3:5,10 (ESV)

The LORD fulfilled **His** intent to **call out against it** by ensuring Jonah reached the city and delivered the message (Jonah 1:2, 3:2).

## The Lasting Result

The miracle resulted in the preservation of more than one hundred and twenty thousand people who did not know their right hand from their left (Jonah 4:11). The king and the people of Nineveh turned from their evil ways and from the violence that was in their hands (Jonah 3:8,10). This repentance delayed the judgment of the city for generations (Jonah 3:10).

Jonah's experience became a sign for future generations (Matthew 12:39-41). Jesus used the "sign of the prophet Jonah" to point toward his own death and resurrection (Matthew 12:40). The story of Jonah taught the Israelites that the LORD is the God of all nations and that **He** desires repentance rather than destruction (Jonah 4:11). It challenged the nationalistic pride of the people of Judah (Jonah 4:2). The result was a lasting testament to the fact that no one is beyond the reach of God's mercy if they turn to **Him** (Jonah 3:10).

## Conclusion

Jonah faced the problem of the wicked city of Nineveh and his own deep-seated prejudice. He was a prophet who tried to limit God's mercy to his own people. However, the LORD promised to use him to warn Nineveh of its evil. Jonah initially rebelled and fled, but God intervened through the storm and the great fish. Jonah eventually obeyed and witnessed the greatest revival in biblical history. The LORD showed that **He** is faithful to **His** mission even when **His** servants are reluctant. Jonah sacrificed his comfort and his pride, but he gained a front-row seat to the power of God's grace. His story proves that we can trust the LORD to accomplish **His** will despite our personal failures. **He** is the God of the second chance **Who** never forsakes **His** calling or **His** creation.
