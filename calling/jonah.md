# Jonah: a Second Chance

A prophet tries to outrun the Presence of the Almighty by crossing the sea in the opposite direction. His flight leads him into the depths of the ocean and the belly of a leviathan. Discover how God's relentless mercy pursues a rebellious servant to save a city.

## The Wickedness of Nineveh
The city of Nineveh was a great and powerful center of the Assyrian Empire (Jonah 1:2). Their evil had come up before the LORD (Jonah 1:2). The Assyrians were known for their extreme violence and cruelty against other nations (Nahum 3:1). Jonah was a Hebrew prophet who feared the God of heaven (Jonah 1:9). He knew that God was gracious and merciful (Jonah 4:2). This knowledge made him reluctant to see his enemies receive a chance to repent (Jonah 4:2). The political environment was one of hostility between Israel and the rising power of Assyria (2 Kings 14:25). The people of Nineveh were on the verge of divine destruction because of their persistent wickedness (Jonah 1:2). The problem was that a great city faced imminent overthrow unless they heard a message of warning they did not deserve (Jonah 3:4).

## Jonah's Sudden Call
Jonah was a prophet of the LORD who had previously served in Israel (2 Kings 14:25). He was the son of Amittai (Jonah 1:1). Jonah did nothing special to get God's attention before his call (Jonah 1:1). He did not fast or offer a specific sacrifice in anticipation of this word (Jonah 1:1-2). He was simply a servant of God to whom the word of the LORD came (Jonah 1:1).

## The Word of the LORD
The word of the LORD came to Jonah directly.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

After his rescue from the sea, the LORD spoke to him a second time.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you**." — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Cost of Obedience
Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and nationalistic pride (Jonah 4:1-2). He risked his reputation as a prophet by preaching a message of judgment that might be averted (Jonah 4:2). He had to abandon his safe life in Israel to travel to a dangerous and hostile foreign capital (Jonah 1:2). He sacrificed his own sense of justice to become an instrument of God's mercy to his enemies (Jonah 4:10-11).

## A Prophet on the Run
Jonah responded with immediate rebellion and flight (Jonah 1:3). He rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship to take him away from his assignment (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went down into the inner part of the ship to hide (Jonah 1:3,5). He distrusted God's plan and tried to escape the divine presence (Jonah 1:10).

## Jonah's Reluctant Reaction
Jonah's initial reaction was to sleep through the storm that God sent to stop him (Jonah 1:4-5). When the sailors cast lots to find the cause of the tempest, the lot fell on him (Jonah 1:7). He confessed that he was a Hebrew who feared the God of heaven Who made the sea and the dry land (Jonah 1:9). He admitted that the storm was his fault (Jonah 1:12). He told the sailors to hurl him into the sea so it would quiet down for them (Jonah 1:12). While in the belly of a great fish, he finally turned back to the LORD in prayer (Jonah 1:17, 2:1). He acknowledged that salvation belongs to the LORD and vowed to sacrifice with a voice of thanksgiving (Jonah 2:9). After being vomited onto dry land, he obeyed the second call and went to Nineveh (Jonah 2:10, 3:3). He walked into the heart of the city and proclaimed that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). He then sat outside the city to see what would become of it (Jonah 4:5). He was still struggling with his anger over God's mercy (Jonah 4:1). He eventually had to face God's correction through the object lesson of a plant that provided shade and a worm that destroyed it (Jonah 4:6-10). He learned that God's compassion extends even to those he considered unworthy of love (Jonah 4:11). His journey showed a slow transition from defiance to reluctant obedience and final understanding of God's heart (Jonah 3:3, 4:11).

## Divine Intervention and Salvation
God used a great fish and the repentance of an entire city to demonstrate His sovereignty and mercy.

> "And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights**." — Jonah 1:17 (ESV)

> "And the LORD spoke to the fish, and **it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land**." — Jonah 2:10 (ESV)

God also worked a miracle in the hearts of the Ninevites as they responded to the warning.

> "And the people of Nineveh believed God. They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them." — Jonah 3:5 (ESV)

> "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it**." — Jonah 3:10 (ESV)

This fulfilled the command that Jonah would **call out against it** and deliver the **message that I tell you** which led to their salvation (Jonah 1:2, 3:2).

## The Harvest of Mercy
The miracle resulted in the survival of over 120,000 people and much cattle (Jonah 4:11). The king and all the citizens turned from their violence and evil ways with fasting and sackcloth (Jonah 3:5,8,10). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD exceedingly (Jonah 1:16). They offered sacrifices to Him and made vows (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself was preserved from death and given a second chance to serve despite his rebellion (Jonah 2:10, 3:1). The story became a sign for future generations of God's power to save and His desire for repentance (Matthew 12:39-41). It demonstrated that God's mercy is not limited by national borders or personal prejudices (Jonah 4:11). The city of Nineveh was spared from the immediate destruction that their sins had invited (Jonah 3:10).

## Conclusion
Jonah faced the problem of a wicked city and his own prejudice. He was a prophet who fled from the LORD'S presence because he disagreed with His mercy. However, the LORD promised to use him to warn Nineveh. Jonah sacrificed his comfort and safety during the storm and his time in the fish. God's intervention through the fish and the Ninevites' repentance proved His faithfulness to His servant and creation. Jonah sacrificed his pride, but he gained a deeper understanding of God's character and witnessed a massive revival. This story proves that even when we fail, we can trust in the LORD'S patience. He pursues the lost and the stubborn alike. Jonah sacrificed his self-righteousness, but he gained a testimony of God's infinite grace.
