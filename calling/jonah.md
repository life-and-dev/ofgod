# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet tries to run away from God's command but finds himself in the belly of a great fish. See how Jonah's escape attempts led him where he did not want to go, and how God's mercy reached an undeserving city.

## The Problem of a Wicked Empire

The city of Nineveh was the capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, known for its extreme cruelty and violence toward other nations (Nahum 3:1). They were enemies of Israel and had a reputation for evil that had reached the presence of God (Jonah 1:2). The political environment was one of dread as Assyria expanded its power through ruthless conquest (Jonah 1:2). The people of Israel hated the Ninevites for their oppression and longed for their destruction (Jonah 4:1-2). The spiritual state of Nineveh was one of total darkness, as they did not know their right hand from their left (Jonah 4:11). The problem was that the wickedness of Nineveh had become so great that divine judgment was imminent unless they repented (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Chosen Condition

Jonah was a prophet, the son of Amittai, when the word of the LORD came to him (Jonah 1:1). He was likely living in Gath-hepher in the territory of Zebulun (2 Kings 14:25). He did not do anything special like praying or fasting to receive this specific calling (Jonah 1:1). He was simply a servant of God to whom the word of the LORD was delivered (Jonah 1:1). He was in a state of nationalistic pride, which later fueled his reluctance to obey (Jonah 4:2).

## The Divine Calling

The LORD spoke to Jonah twice. First, to command him to go to Nineveh, and second, to repeat the command after his rescue.

> Now the word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, "**Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me." — Jonah 1:1-2 (ESV)

After Jonah was vomited out by the fish:

> Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time, saying, "**Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it the message that I tell you.**" — Jonah 3:1-2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice

Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and his nationalistic reputation among his own people (Jonah 1:3). He risked being seen as a traitor for offering a chance of repentance to Israel's most brutal enemies (Jonah 4:1-2). He sacrificed his own desire for vengeance against those who oppressed his nation (Jonah 4:2). He also risked his life by traveling to a hostile and violent foreign capital to deliver a message of doom (Jonah 3:3-4).

## Jonah's Initial Response

Jonah immediately rebelled and tried to flee from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish, paying the fare to go in the opposite direction of Nineveh (Jonah 1:3). He thought he could escape his calling by leaving the land of Israel (Jonah 1:3). When a great storm threatened the ship, he went into the inner part of the ship and fell fast asleep, showing his indifference (Jonah 1:5).

## Jonah's Complicated Reaction

Eventually, Jonah realized he could not escape the hand of the LORD (Jonah 1:12). After being thrown into the sea at his own request to save the sailors, he was swallowed by a great fish (Jonah 1:12,17). From the belly of the fish, Jonah prayed and acknowledged his dependence on the LORD (Jonah 2:1-9). He recognized that salvation belongs to the LORD (Jonah 2:9). After the fish vomited him onto dry land, he obeyed the second call and went to Nineveh (Jonah 3:3). He walked through the city and cried out the message of judgment (Jonah 3:4).

However, even after the Ninevites repented, Jonah's heart was still not aligned with God's mercy (Jonah 4:1). He became angry that God spared the city and complained that he knew God was gracious and merciful (Jonah 4:2). He even asked the LORD to take his life because he was so displeased with the outcome (Jonah 4:3). He sat outside the city to see what would happen, still hoping for its destruction (Jonah 4:5). The LORD used a plant, a worm, and a scorching wind to teach Jonah about compassion (Jonah 4:6-8). Jonah showed more concern for the plant that gave him shade than for the thousands of people in Nineveh (Jonah 4:10-11). Despite his failures and his bad attitude, Jonah's reaction was ultimately one of submission to God's sovereign plan (Jonah 3:3). Jonah proved that God can use a reluctant and even resentful messenger to accomplish **His** purposes (Jonah 3:10). Jonah's story was preserved as a testimony against his own prejudice (Jonah 4:11).

## The Divine Miracle

The LORD performed several miracles to direct Jonah and bring Nineveh to repentance. **He** hurled a great wind upon the sea and appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah (Jonah 1:4,17).

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights. — Jonah 1:17 (ESV)

> And the LORD spoke to the fish, and **it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land.** — Jonah 2:10 (ESV)

The greatest miracle was the total repentance of Nineveh from the king down to the animals (Jonah 3:5-9). This fulfilled God's purpose to **call out against it** and bring about a change (Jonah 3:10).

> When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster** that **He** had said **He** would do to them, and **He** did not do it. — Jonah 3:10 (ESV)

**He** also appointed a plant, a worm, and a wind to discipline Jonah (Jonah 4:6-8).

## The Lasting Result

The miracle resulted in the preservation of Nineveh and its inhabitants who were spared from destruction (Jonah 3:10). The king of Nineveh led a national fast and turned the people from their evil ways and violence (Jonah 3:7-8). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD, offered sacrifices, and made vows (Jonah 1:16). For Jonah, the result was a humbling lesson on the breadth of God's mercy toward all nations (Jonah 4:11). The story of Jonah became a sign for future generations, eventually being used by Jesus to point to his own death and resurrection (Matthew 12:39-41). It challenged Israel's exclusive view of God's favor and demonstrated that God desires the repentance of even the most wicked people (Jonah 4:11).

## Conclusion

Jonah faced the problem of Nineveh's wickedness and his own deep-seated hatred for his enemies. He was limited by his prejudice and his attempt to flee from God's presence. However, the LORD promised to use him to warn the city. Jonah responded first with rebellion, then with reluctant obedience, and finally with resentment. God intervened by sending a storm, a fish, and a message that moved an entire empire to repent. The result was the salvation of a city and a permanent record of God's universal compassion. Jonah sacrificed his pride and his comfort, but he gained the honor of being a sign of God's mercy. His story proves that we can trust the LORD'S faithfulness even when we are unfaithful or reluctant. **He** is the God of the second chance **Who** never forsakes **His** purpose or **His** people.
