# Jonah: the Reluctant Prophet

A prophet receives a command to warn a wicked city of impending doom. He chooses to run in the opposite direction, hoping to escape his divine assignment. This journey through the depths of the sea reveals the surprising extent of God's mercy.

## The Problem of Pagan Wickedness
Jonah was the son of Amittai and a prophet from Gath-hepher (2 Kings 14:25). He lived during the reign of Jeroboam II when Israel was expanding its borders (2 Kings 14:23-25). However, the Assyrian empire was a rising power known for its extreme cruelty (Nahum 3:1,19). Nineveh was the great capital of this empire (Jonah 1:2). The people of Nineveh were famous for their violence and wickedness (Jonah 1:2; Nahum 3:1). Their evil had become so great that it required divine intervention (Jonah 1:2). The problem was that the people of Nineveh faced certain destruction because of their persistent wickedness (Jonah 1:2, 3:4).

## Jonah's Normal Condition
Jonah was a recognized prophet in Israel who had previously spoken words from the LORD (2 Kings 14:25). He did not do anything specific like fasting or visiting the temple to seek this particular word (Jonah 1:1). The word of the LORD simply came to him while he was in his normal state (Jonah 1:1). He was a servant of the LORD, the God of heaven (Jonah 1:9).

## The Divine Calling
The word of the LORD came to Jonah with a clear command to warn Nineveh.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it, for their evil has come up before Me.**" — Jonah 1:2 (ESV)

Later, after Jonah's attempt to flee, the LORD spoke a second time.

> "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you.**" — Jonah 3:2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice
Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and national pride (Jonah 1:3, 4:1-2). He risked his reputation as a prophet in Israel to travel to a dangerous enemy city (Jonah 3:3). He risked his life by entering a city known for its violence (Nahum 3:1). He also had to sacrifice his own desire to see his enemies destroyed (Jonah 4:2).

## Jonah's Initial Response
Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish (Jonah 1:3). He paid the fare and went down into the ship (Jonah 1:3). He attempted to run away from his divine assignment because he knew God was merciful (Jonah 4:2).

## Jonah's Reluctant Reaction
After being rescued from the fish, Jonah obeyed the word of the LORD and went to Nineveh (Jonah 3:3). Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, three days' journey in breadth (Jonah 3:3). Jonah began to go into the city, going a day's journey (Jonah 3:4). He cried out that in forty days Nineveh would be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). The people of Nineveh believed God and called for a fast (Jonah 3:5). They put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them (Jonah 3:5). Even the king of Nineveh arose from his throne, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes (Jonah 3:6). The king issued a proclamation for everyone to turn from their evil ways and violence (Jonah 3:7-8). Jonah witnessed a city-wide repentance (Jonah 3:10). However, he then went out of the city and sat to the east to see what would happen (Jonah 4:5). He still struggled with his own anger and frustration over God's mercy (Jonah 4:1-3).

## The Divine Miracle
God performed a miracle by preserving Jonah in the sea and turning the hearts of an entire pagan city.

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.** — Jonah 1:17 (ESV)

> And the LORD spoke to the fish, and **it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land.** — Jonah 2:10 (ESV)

> When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them, and He did not do it.** — Jonah 3:10 (ESV)

The LORD fulfilled His intent to **call out against it** so that they might repent and be spared (Jonah 1:2).

## The Global Result
The miracle resulted in the salvation of more than one hundred and twenty thousand people (Jonah 4:11). The entire city of Nineveh was spared from destruction (Jonah 3:10). Jonah himself was humbled and forced to confront his own lack of compassion (Jonah 4:10-11). The repentance of the Ninevites stood as a witness to that generation (Matthew 12:41). The story of Jonah became a sign for future generations (Matthew 12:39-40). It demonstrated that God's mercy extends to all nations, not just Israel (Jonah 4:11).

## Conclusion
Jonah faced the problem of Nineveh's wickedness and his own deep-seated prejudice. He was a prophet who failed initially by fleeing from his calling. However, the LORD promised to judge the city through Jonah's message. Jonah responded with rebellion and then reluctant obedience. God intervened by sending a storm, a great fish, and a message of repentance. The result was the preservation of a massive city and a lesson in divine mercy. Jonah sacrificed his pride and his hatred, but he gained a witness to the vastness of God's grace. This story proves that we can trust the LORD to be faithful to His character. He is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.
