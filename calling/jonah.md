# Jonah: Reluctant Messenger

A prophet is sent to his nation's greatest enemy to deliver a message of doom. Instead of obeying, he boards a ship to the opposite end of the world. Discover how God's relentless mercy pursues a rebellious servant through a storm and a great fish.

## The Problem of a Cruel Empire
Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian Empire and a city of immense wickedness (Jonah 1:2, 3:3). The Assyrians were notorious for their extreme cruelty and violence toward their enemies, including the people of Israel (Nahum 3:1,19). Their evil had come up before the LORD, demanding a divine response (Jonah 1:2). As a prophet of Israel, Jonah likely viewed the Assyrians as a direct threat to his own nation and its survival (2 Kings 14:25). The political environment was one of constant fear and oppression for smaller nations under the Assyrian shadow (Nahum 3:1-4). The problem was that a massive, violent city was on the brink of total destruction unless they heard a message from a God they did not know (Jonah 3:4).

## Jonah's Status as Prophet
Jonah was a recognized prophet in Israel, the son of Amittai (Jonah 1:1). He had previously prophesied successfully during the reign of Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25). Jonah did nothing special to get God's attention like fasting or praying before the word of the LORD came to him (Jonah 1:1). He was simply a servant of the LORD already established in his ministry (Jonah 1:1,9).

## The Divine Command
The LORD spoke to Jonah to send him on a mission of warning to the enemies of his people.

> Now the word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it**, for their evil has come up before Me."
> — Jonah 1:1-2 (ESV)

After his first attempt to flee, the LORD spoke to Jonah a second time.

> Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time, saying, "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and **call out against it the message that I tell you**."
> — Jonah 3:1-2 (ESV)

## The Necessary Sacrifice
Jonah had to sacrifice his personal comfort and his nationalistic pride (Jonah 1:3, 4:2). By going to Nineveh, he risked his reputation as a prophet of Israel and his physical safety among a notoriously violent people (Nahum 3:1). He feared that God's mercy toward Nineveh would lead to the eventual destruction of his own nation (Jonah 4:2). He sacrificed his desire for justice against his enemies to serve as an instrument of God's grace (Jonah 4:1-3).

## Running from God
Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD (Jonah 1:3). He went down to Joppa and found a ship going to Tarshish, paying the fare to escape his assignment (Jonah 1:3). He chose to travel in the exact opposite direction of Nineveh (Jonah 1:3). Even when a great storm threatened the ship, Jonah went to the inner part of the vessel and fell fast asleep (Jonah 1:5). He initially preferred death over obeying God's command (Jonah 1:12).

## Preaching in the Enemy City
After being swallowed by the fish, Jonah prayed to the LORD his God from its belly (Jonah 2:1). He acknowledged that the LORD had cast him into the deep and that his prayer had come into His holy temple (Jonah 2:3,7). He vowed to sacrifice to the LORD with the voice of thanksgiving (Jonah 2:9). When the fish vomited him onto dry land, the word of the LORD came to him a second time (Jonah 2:10, 3:1). Jonah arose and went to Nineveh according to the word of the LORD (Jonah 3:3). He entered the exceedingly great city and began to proclaim that it would be overthrown in forty days (Jonah 3:4). Even though he was angry when the people repented, he still fulfilled the task of delivering the message (Jonah 3:5, 4:1). He sat east of the city in a booth to see what would become of it (Jonah 4:5). Although his heart was still struggling with God's compassion, his outward actions were compliant with the divine command (Jonah 3:3, 4:1-2). He accepted the LORD'S correction regarding his lack of pity for the city and the plant (Jonah 4:10-11).

## The Divine Miracle
God performed a series of miracles to save both Nineveh and His reluctant prophet. He sent a great wind, appointed a great fish, and later caused a city-wide repentance.

> And the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And **Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights**.
> — Jonah 1:17 (ESV)

> And the LORD spoke to the fish, and **it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land**.
> — Jonah 2:10 (ESV)

> When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, **God relented of the disaster that He had said He would do to them**.
> — Jonah 3:10 (ESV)

In these events, God fulfilled His promise to have Jonah **call out against it** by ensuring he reached Nineveh despite his rebellion (Jonah 1:2).

## A City Spared
The miracle resulted in the salvation of more than one hundred and twenty thousand people in Nineveh (Jonah 4:11). The king and all the citizens turned from their evil ways and violence, leading God to spare the city (Jonah 3:8-10). The sailors on the ship also came to fear the LORD exceedingly, offering sacrifices and making vows (Jonah 1:16). Jonah himself learned a profound lesson about the depth of God's steadfast love and mercy for all nations (Jonah 4:10-11). The story became a sign for future generations, including the time of Jesus (Matthew 12:39-41).

## Conclusion
Jonah faced the problem of a wicked enemy city and his own stubborn refusal to help them. He was a prophet limited by his own prejudice and desire for personal comfort. However, the LORD promised to use him to warn Nineveh. Jonah sacrificed his safety and his pride after initially fleeing into a storm. God intervened by preserving him in a great fish and sovereignly turning the hearts of the Ninevites. The result was a massive spiritual awakening and the preservation of a great city. Jonah sacrificed his comfort and his reputation, but he gained a firsthand experience of God's overwhelming grace and the privilege of being a witness to a miracle of repentance. This story demonstrates that we can trust the LORD even in our failures, for He is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.
